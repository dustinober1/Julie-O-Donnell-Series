Forward Post Arjun
Kashmir
17:22 Indian Standard Time

The storm had thinned enough to show the mountain, which was worse.

Major Ananya Sharma stood at the observation slit and looked at ground she had spent eleven winters learning not to trust when she could see it. Cloud had lifted off the western approaches sometime after the guns went cold. What remained was a grey corridor of rock and old snow running east from Sector Zebra-Nine toward a ridge line the fire plan had never mentioned, because until three hours ago no product had put anything on it.

Lieutenant Sameer Qureshi brought the supplemental page to the slit rather than the console. He had learned that much in a day.

"It came through the same route as the withdrawal," he said. "Categories only. No geography beyond the sector we already hold."

Sharma read it twice. The American source had failed. That she knew from her own screen, from red symbols dividing into copies of themselves and a confidence value that walked down to nothing while her battery commanders waited on the net. The supplemental added something the first result had not contained. Low-level events had existed inside the contaminated feed and had been recovered afterward. Brief thermal contacts. Weight transfers too light for vehicles. Communications bursts sitting under the false carrier.

They had surfaced late. The page said so plainly, in a line she read a third time.

ADDITIONAL DELAY ATTRIBUTABLE TO ANALYTIC SCOPE: 43 SECONDS.

"Someone wrote that about themselves," Qureshi said.

"Yes."

"They did not have to."

"No."

It was the first thing out of Washington in two days that Sharma had believed without checking. It did not make the events on her mountain smaller. Five or six people, spaced so that no two of them ever occupied the same sensor field, moving east while every instrument in two countries looked at an artillery formation that had never existed. The last of them had been observed a little over two kilometres from Relay K-17, and that observation was now nine hours old.

She had held fire on her own authority. Nobody had thanked her for it and nobody would. The corollary arrived without ceremony: the same authority obliged her to find out what her sector actually contained, and it obliged her today, before the ground froze over whatever was on it.

"Captain Rao," she said.

Arvind Rao had been waiting outside the report room since the batteries safed, which meant he had worked out the order before she gave it. He came in with his cold-weather layers already on and stopped at the map table.

"K-17," he said.

"K-17. Point Kestrel first."

Naib Subedar Vikram Sethi followed him in and stood at the end of the table without being asked. He was older than Rao and broader through the shoulders, and the relay-maintenance badge in the transparent pocket on his chest was the reason he was in the room at all. Sharma addressed him before she addressed the patrol.

"Are you the registered custodian for that relay?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Then say what that means and what it does not."

Sethi considered the question long enough to answer it properly. "It means I can release the local incident cartridge from the exterior cassette without opening the relay, without a network path, and without touching the journal that is already written. It does not mean I can tell you what is on it. The isolation cassette is on the safe side of the boundary. Everything that would tell you whether the relay was used is inside."

"Could someone else have released it?"

"Not without the registration. They could have destroyed the medium."

"Could they have written to it?"

"It is append-only. They could add. They could not remove."

Sharma turned the map so the light from the slit fell across the eastern corridor. "Then the cartridge comes back and the relay stays where it is."

Rao's team assembled in the vehicle bay while the light went. Five men. Rao, Sethi, Lance Naik Suresh Pal, and two riflemen from the post's protection element who had drawn the task by being the two least tired. They carried thermal optics, glass, anchor line, a steel transit box with a chain, and a field sleeve for the cartridge. They did not carry a reader. Sharma had refused it twice.

Qureshi opened the standalone register and wrote the patrol in against Arjun's wall clock, because the allied network was not going to be told anything by this post until Sharma understood what it had.

She gave the brief herself, in the bay, with the doors open and the cold coming in off the switchback road.

"You are going to look at a relay," she said. "You are not going to take it, clear it, or defend it. Captain Rao holds the patrol. Naib Subedar Sethi holds the cartridge. Nobody else touches either."

Rao repeated it back. Sharma let him finish and then gave him the part that mattered.

"Kestrel before approach. You will observe from Kestrel for as long as it takes to be certain of what you are walking into, and if that is longer than you like, it is still shorter than a funeral. When you move, you move in pairs with the mast between you and the ridge. You never form one group."

"Understood, ma'am."

"Three stops." She held up her hand and did not lower it until she had counted all three onto her fingers. "You do not cross the inner boundary if you cannot exclude that someone is inside it. You do not cross it if you cannot exclude a tamper or explosive hazard on the lower seam. And you do not follow tracks onto ground where a crossing of the Line of Control cannot be excluded. Any one of those, you stop. Not all three. Any one."

"And if the relay has been opened?"

"Then it has been opened and you will photograph the outside of it and come home. Captain, I am not sending you to find out who. I am sending you to find out whether."

Rao held her eye. "Brigade will ask who."

"Brigade will ask me."

Pal was checking the anchor line at the tailgate and pretending not to listen, in the way of a man listening carefully. He was twenty-four and had spent the previous night on the guns. Sharma stopped beside him on her way out of the bay.

"You were on Three."

"Yes, ma'am. One tube loaded. Firing circuit safe."

"You reported it correctly."

"It was already true, ma'am."

She almost let that go. "It was true on Three because you kept it true. That is not the same sentence and I would like you to know the difference before you climb."

Pal looked at the line in his hands. "Yes, ma'am."

They went out at 17:51 with the last of the useful light behind them, five men against a corridor of rock that the machine had not been able to see and that no one at Arjun could see either. Sharma watched them onto the second switchback and then went back to the observation room, because the alternative was watching until she could not distinguish them from the ground.

Qureshi had put the fire plan away. The console still carried the withdrawn product, the contaminated-source warning, and the counter-battery package sitting grey and invalid where it had been armed.

"Ma'am. Northern Command."

"Tell them the sector is quiet."

"Is it?"

Sharma looked at the eastern corridor, empty as far as any instrument at the post could establish, and at the clock that had begun to run on five men walking into it.

"It is quiet," she said. "That is a different thing from empty, and I would like the record to say which one I told them."

Qureshi wrote it down that way.
