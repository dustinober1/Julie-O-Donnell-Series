# Chapter 1 — A Written Boundary

The visitor badge on Julie O’Donnell’s jacket said ESCORT REQUIRED in letters large enough to settle any question about her standing.

She left it visible.

At 10:32 on Wednesday morning, the briefing room at the Canadian joint response center held six people, three sealed folders, and one proposed mandate that gave her more authority than anyone in the room intended. Rain tracked down the narrow windows behind Inspector Amrita Dhaliwal. Beyond them, low cloud had swallowed the Interior British Columbia ridgeline. Inside, the provincial duty director kept glancing at a muted television where the withdrawn spillway warning still crawled beneath footage of closed roads.

“We need a defensible explanation before the noon briefing,” he said. “Legacy failover remains the most likely cause.”

Julie read the second page again. The mandate authorized her to examine all records related to the warning and subsequent continuity actions. Those five words reached past the dam records, past the withdrawn release recommendation, and into a protected movement file that had not been placed on the table.

She set the page down. “I can’t sign this.”

The duty director’s chair moved against the floor. “You haven’t seen the evidence.”

“That’s the problem. This makes me responsible for records you haven’t identified and conclusions you haven’t authorized me to test.”

Dhaliwal had not offered Julie a pen. The RCMP inspector sat across from her with both hands beside the blue mandate folder, guarding it as carefully as the evidence cases stacked against the wall.

“What language would you accept?” she asked.

“The exact question first.”

“Whether the public-safety warning resulted from a source-integrity failure.”

“That could mean a bad sensor, a bad conversion, a bad assumption, or misconduct. Which one am I being asked to prove?”

“You are not being asked to prove misconduct.”

“Then the mandate should say so.”

Utility counsel shifted a paper copy toward Dhaliwal but kept two fingers on the corner. The originals remained at the dam under the utility’s control. The room held certified views and custodian-produced extracts, each carrying its own source label and clock basis. Julie had requested that separation before boarding the flight west. Seeing it observed made her more willing to argue over the rest.

They revised the question to whether the normalized action sequence was supported by the identified source records. The plant retained its originals. RCMP retained its continuity records. Julie could examine certified views on Canadian-controlled systems, request comparisons, record findings, and recommend preservation. She could not copy, download, remove, seize, compel, direct an interview, or open another repository.

The duty director leaned forward. “If the purpose changes, we lose another hour rewriting paper.”

“If the purpose changes, the paper is the only way everyone knows whose risk changed with it.”

Dhaliwal watched her. “Who authorizes expansion?”

“The lawful custodian for the new source and the official responsible for the investigation. Both named. Legal review if the disclosure boundary changes.”

“And if a custodian objects?”

“The objection travels with the finding. They can narrow or stop access to what they own. You decide what Canadian investigative step follows.”

“You don’t.”

“No.”

The answer came without difficulty. That seemed to interest Dhaliwal more than any claim of expertise would have.

A disclosure clause limited the work to the protected review team. A proof-limit clause barred conclusions about actor, intent, or deliberate manipulation unless later evidence supported them. The missing scientist remained outside Julie’s assignment. If her status became relevant, Dhaliwal would have to tender a defined record and request a written scope change.

Julie added one final sentence: if the review’s purpose, operational use, or foreseeable risk changed, the named authority had to decide whether the work continued.

The duty director read it and frowned. “That gives every participant a brake.”

“It gives every participant a name beside the brake.”

At 11:03, Dhaliwal signed for the RCMP. Utility counsel signed as source custodian. The duty director signed for the emergency coordination authority. Only then did Dhaliwal pass Julie the pen.

Julie signed carefully beneath the line stating that she held no Canadian police power and no independent right of access. The long flight had stiffened her right wrist, making each letter deliberate.

Dhaliwal closed the folder. “Now I’ll show you the clocks.”

* * *

The evidence room had no windows and no loose paper.

A utility records custodian controlled the terminal from the far side of the table. Julie sat where she could see the screen but could not reach the keyboard. Dhaliwal remained standing, one shoulder against a cabinet marked with a red evidence seal. The duty director had followed them and brought the failover explanation with him.

The custodian opened four certified source views: actuator current, gate position, structural vibration, and downstream hydraulic response. Each carried a device identifier, local clock basis, last synchronization state, and extraction signature.

Then she opened the normalized display that had faced the operators before dawn.

The platform arranged the records into a clean emergency: high inflow, an actuator-current spike, delayed gate movement, rising structural vibration, then the downstream response expected from unstable spillway control. The sequence justified a controlled drawdown, road closures, evacuation of exposed lowlands, and preparation for an emergency release.

It also placed the downstream effect after the gate movement that supposedly caused it.

Julie asked the custodian to hide the normalized timestamps.

The duty director looked at her. “Those are the common times.”

“They’re the platform’s common times. Show me what each device recorded before conversion.”

The custodian brought forward the source-native values. Their formats differed. One counted from a controller restart. Two used local time with separate synchronization records. The downstream gauge carried its own disciplined clock and transmission delay. None could be compared honestly by lining up the displayed seconds and pretending the labels meant the same thing.

Julie requested the synchronization certificates and the known delay bounds. The custodian opened them one at a time. Julie wrote nothing. She held the relationships in her head until the custodian produced a temporary comparison strip on the screen, with uncertainty bands visible instead of compressed into single points.

The order changed.

The actuator-current spike remained authentic. The gate-position lag remained authentic. So did the vibration and the downstream hydraulic response. High inflow made each event consequential, but it did not supply the missing order between them. The source clocks placed the downstream response before the gate movement that the normalized display used as its cause. The documented clock uncertainty could widen the interval. It could not reverse it.

“Run the failover correction,” the duty director said.

The custodian selected the legacy-gateway model. It applied the vendor’s documented reconnect offsets and packet-delay assumptions. The bands shifted. The impossible order remained.

“Again with the maximum allowable delay.”

The second run moved the points farther apart.

Julie looked at the operator log. The veteran duty manager had challenged the release recommendation before the platform completed its own reconciliation. His entry used plain language: DOWNSTREAM RESPONSE PRECEDES COMMANDABLE GATE CHANGE ON SOURCE CLOCKS. HOLD IRREVERSIBLE RELEASE.

“Could the downstream gauge be wrong?” Dhaliwal asked.

“It could. The current record doesn’t show that it is.”

“The gate-position sensor?”

“Same answer.”

The duty director tapped the normalized sequence. “Something failed.”

“Yes. We have not established what.”

“We have a platform failover during high inflow.”

“We have a failover and an unsupported causal order. Putting them in the same sentence does not prove one caused the other.”

His jaw tightened. “The public needs reassurance.”

“The public needs an accurate statement. You can say the warning was withdrawn because the normalized sequence was unsupported by the source chronology. You can call failover a hypothesis under review. You can’t promote it to a finding because it is ordinary enough to be comforting.”

Dhaliwal asked the custodian to display the extraction signatures again. Each source record remained internally coherent with its own operating history. No unexplained edit appeared between device capture and certified extraction. The first unsupported layer was the relationship imposed by normalization.

Julie phrased the finding once. Dhaliwal made her narrow “contradicted” to “unsupported by” because the downstream gauge had not yet been independently validated. Julie accepted the correction.

The inspector entered the sentence herself.

The records custodian sealed the comparison session. Only after the seal registered did she mention a restricted continuity notation involving the scientist who had helped design the binational warning architecture.

Dhaliwal looked at Julie. “She is outside your mandate.”

“Yes.”

“I’m deciding whether that changes.”

* * *

At 11:54, Dhaliwal carried the question to an RCMP records station on the other side of the operations floor.

The station was separated from the utility network by a locked glass door. Dhaliwal authenticated alone and read the access notice aloud for the recording. Julie remained behind a yellow line while the inspector opened the restricted index.

“The scientist had scheduled access to the source systems?” Julie asked.

“She had authorized review responsibilities. That is all I’m prepared to establish here.”

“Then don’t add her status to the source finding.”

Dhaliwal turned from the monitor. “You think the records are unrelated?”

“I think relation is what we would be testing. The mandate doesn’t let me begin with the answer.”

They returned to the briefing room long enough to add a bounded scope change. Julie could assess what one specified continuity record established about the scientist’s recorded transfer and whether that record identified a receiving acceptance. She received no missing-person mandate, interview authority, or access to the broader protective file. The RCMP retained every investigative decision.

Back at the station, Dhaliwal opened the record.

The first line showed that protected movement had been authorized. The authority code validated against the Canadian registry. The second line closed the local transportation stage. A vehicle reference, departure time, and Canadian custodian entry were present.

The third line read: TRANSFERRED TO PARTNER CUSTODY.

The sentence ended there.

Dhaliwal enlarged the metadata pane. The record carried a valid Canadian signature, a restricted routing code, and a completed-status flag. It had no receiving officer, receiving organization, acceptance time, custody receipt, or partner-generated reference visible within the authorized view.

“Those fields may be protected,” she said.

“They may be.”

“The routing code may resolve them in another compartment.”

“It may.”

“You’re being careful with my possibilities and not with your own.”

Julie stepped closer to the yellow line without crossing it. “My conclusion is smaller than yours. This proves Canadian officials opened protected movement, recorded the local stage as closed, and claimed transfer to partner custody. It doesn’t show acceptance in the record we are authorized to see.”

Dhaliwal’s expression hardened. “Closing a transport stage without a handoff would be a serious failure.”

“Then don’t infer that failure before you know whether the receipt exists elsewhere.”

The records officer joined them, remaining on Dhaliwal’s side of the line. She confirmed that the Canadian entry had passed its required administrative checks. The system allowed closure when an authorized transfer code was entered. A receiving reference could be attached later or held separately under partner control.

“Could the Canadian custodian have witnessed the receiving officer take physical custody?” Dhaliwal asked.

“The form does not require the witness account in this field.”

“Could the transfer have been remote, administrative, or staged through another Canadian custodian?”

“The code does not distinguish those possibilities in this view.”

The Canadian signature authenticated the Canadian entry. It did not authenticate an unseen partner’s acceptance.

Julie asked Dhaliwal to separate the record into propositions. The inspector resisted the phrasing, then did it herself on a fresh RCMP note: protected movement opened; Canadian local stage recorded closed; transfer to partner custody claimed; partner acceptance not demonstrated in the available record.

“Do you want ‘missing’?” Dhaliwal asked.

“No. Missing from this view could become missing everywhere by the time it reaches a briefing.”

“Unverified?”

“That can sound as though the Canadian entry failed authentication. It didn’t.”

Dhaliwal considered the screen. “Receiving acceptance not demonstrated.”

“Yes.”

The inspector placed the exact words beneath the Canadian record rather than inside it.

At 12:33, she opened the secure liaison request form.

“What question?” she asked.

Julie looked at the claimed transfer line. “Whether the designated American receiving records show a corresponding acceptance under this transfer reference, identity, and time window.”

“Nothing broader?”

“Nothing broader.”

Dhaliwal submitted the request through the RCMP liaison route. The request asked whether the designated American records contained the receiving act the Canadian entry claimed.

* * *

The secure liaison desk faced a blank wall.

There was no shared search portal, no binational evidence pool, and no screen Julie could touch. Dhaliwal controlled the Canadian request. An American liaison officer controlled what could be checked and what could be returned. At 12:41, the officer appeared by encrypted video and repeated the limits before accepting the identifiers.

“I can query the designated acceptance registry and the receiving office log named in your request,” he said. “I cannot confirm or deny unrelated protected holdings. I cannot search by theory, affiliation, or an undisclosed alternate identity.”

Dhaliwal said, “Understood.”

Julie remained outside the camera frame until the officer asked who had formulated the source question. Dhaliwal identified her as a mandate-bound analytical adviser with no repository access. The officer requested the written scope statement, received it through the RCMP channel, and began the check.

The duty director arrived twelve minutes later. “How long does a receipt search take?”

“As long as the receiving authority requires,” Dhaliwal said.

“We have a briefing at thirteen-thirty.”

“Then brief what we know at thirteen-thirty.”

He looked toward Julie. “If the Americans have no record, we have a disappearance.”

“We have a transfer claim under review.”

“Everyone keeps choosing smaller words.”

“Smaller words survive contact with evidence.”

The liaison officer returned at 13:11 with a written response. Dhaliwal opened it on the Canadian terminal and read it aloud.

No corresponding acceptance or custody record had been located in the two authorized repositories under the supplied transfer reference, identity information, and bounded time window. The response did not address unrelated holdings, alternate authorities, protected compartments not named in the request, records under materially different identifiers, or receipt outside the stated period.

The duty director exhaled. “So the United States never took her.”

Dhaliwal answered before Julie could. “That is not what he said.”

The officer confirmed the limitation. He would not identify what other repositories existed or whether another authority could hold a responsive record. A broader request required its own legal basis and named decision-maker. Dhaliwal thanked him, closed the channel, and retained the signed response inside the RCMP system.

Julie read the Canadian transfer claim on one screen and the bounded American negative on the other. Neither authenticated the other. Together they established a break between claimed release and demonstrated acceptance. The gap could support a broader inquiry; it could not choose the inquiry’s answer.

Dhaliwal opened the investigative note.

“Abduction?” the duty director asked.

“No,” she said.

“Missing person?”

“Already being handled under Canadian authority. This note concerns custody.”

She typed: CUSTODY GAP — CORRESPONDING RECEIVING ACCEPTANCE NOT LOCATED WITHIN AUTHORIZED CHECK. FURTHER INQUIRY REQUIRED.

Julie asked her to add the repositories and time window. Dhaliwal did. She also attached the American limitations and the Canadian custodian’s objection to any language implying misconduct. The original movement record remained unchanged.

The wall clock reached 13:18.

Dhaliwal removed her authentication card. “Her family was told she entered protected movement. They have not been given a receiving authority.”

“And now?”

“Now I speak to them as the Canadian investigator. You may attend under the amended mandate. You do not question them unless I authorize it, and you do not describe this as an abduction.”

“I wouldn’t.”

“I needed it on the record.”

Julie looked once more at the transfer line. It used the past tense with the confidence of a completed act. The receiving side had supplied no matching hand within the only search they had lawfully made.

The scientist’s family would ask where she was. Julie could tell them why the official sentence no longer closed the question. She could not tell them how the sentence had been completed, who had relied on it, or what had happened after the Canadian record stopped.

Dhaliwal held the door.

Julie left the screens behind and carried only the boundary.
